Pre-Deployment IMU Validation Experiment Using a Fixed-Rotation Swimming Toy

Featured image: A close view of one active window. The gyroscope Z-axis and accelerometer X-axis both preserve the toy's periodic rotation.

Background and Motivation

Before strapping the IMU logger onto a real swimmer's wrist for a full training session, I did not want the first underwater test to gamble an entire session on whether the hardware would work. Instead, I mounted the device on a fixed-rotation swimming toy and ran a low-cost, low-risk pilot experiment in the pool.

The toy made it possible to test repeatedly without coordinating a swimmer's schedule. More importantly, it let me answer three practical questions before deployment:

  • Would the 3D-printed enclosure, IMU, and SD-card system remain reliable through repeated submersion?
  • Would the logger preserve a complete recording without dropped frames, corruption, or underwater disconnections?
  • What would a simple periodic motion look like at the actual sensor sampling rate?

This experiment follows the earlier IMU Data Acquisition and Storage Experiment Based on Seeed XIAO and JY901, which had validated the same hardware setup on land.

Experiment Design

  • Device: Seeed XIAO, JY901 9-axis IMU, and an SD-card module inside a 3D-printed enclosure.
  • Motion source: A swimming toy that produces repetitive rotational motion.
  • Scenario: The toy was repeatedly started, rotated, paused, submerged, and returned to the surface.
  • Recording: The full session, including quiet intervals, was retained so that both motion and resting-state data could be checked.

Figure 1: The IMU module was fixed to the toy's waist with a bracket and connected by wires.

Figure 2: The toy was controlled with a long pole and repeatedly rotated at and below the water surface.

The final log contains 6,765 rows spanning 871.5 seconds, or about 14.5 minutes. During that time, the toy completed 15 distinct active windows separated by clear resting intervals.

Data Completeness and Device Reliability

Figure 3: Gyroscope magnitude across the full 871-second recording. Green shading marks the 15 automatically identified active windows.

The full recording showed no missing values or abnormal timing jumps. The median sampling interval was approximately 129 ms, corresponding to 7.75 Hz, with a standard deviation of only 3.8 ms. Serial communication and SD-card writes therefore remained stable through repeated submersion and surfacing, and the enclosure showed no evidence of water ingress.

That result clears the two most immediate deployment risks: the hardware survived a long underwater session, and the complete acquisition-to-storage path remained intact.

It also exposed an important discrepancy. The measured rate of about 7.75 Hz is much lower than the 50–100 Hz discussed in the earlier hardware test. The firmware and logging configuration should be checked before using the device for finer-grained stroke-style recognition.

Preliminary Periodicity Analysis

The featured plot at the top of this post zooms in on one active interval, from roughly 460 to 520 seconds. Both the gyroscope Z-axis and accelerometer X-axis show recognizable periodic waveforms, confirming that the IMU captures the toy's rotational rhythm even at the measured sampling rate.

Each cycle was estimated by detecting peaks in the combined gyroscope magnitude:

from scipy.signal import find_peaks
import numpy as np

def cycle_periods(t, sig, min_period_s, max_period_s):
    dt = np.median(np.diff(t))
    distance = max(1, int(round(min_period_s / dt)))
    prominence = np.std(sig) * 0.5
    peaks, _ = find_peaks(sig, distance=distance,
                          prominence=prominence)
    if len(peaks) < 4:
        return None
    periods = np.diff(t[peaks])
    keep = (periods >= min_period_s) & (periods <= max_period_s)
    return periods[keep]

Figure 4: Rotation rate and timing consistency for each of the 15 active windows.

The estimated rotation rate ranged from 63 to 122 cycles per minute, with a median near 91 cycles per minute, equivalent to a period of about 0.66 seconds. The period coefficient of variation (CV) ranged from 0.22 to 0.46, with a median around 0.35.

What the Baseline Taught Me

The rate range is in the same broad region as the 57–81 strokes per minute observed in a real-swimmer dataset. This provides an initial practical constraint for a future sliding-window detector: a window should cover at least 1.5–2 cycles, or roughly 1–2 seconds, to contain a complete motion pattern.

The timing variability was the more surprising result. I had expected a fixed mechanical rotation to produce a CV close to zero. Instead, the median CV was 0.35. This shows that the combined sensor and peak-detection pipeline has a non-trivial noise floor; even a simple mechanical motion does not look perfectly regular after measurement and processing.

That changes how the toy data should be used. It is not a zero-noise model of an ideal swimming stroke, and it cannot be subtracted from real-swimmer motion to reveal technique defects. Its stronger role is as a measurement baseline: it helps separate variability introduced by sensing and detection from variability that may genuinely reflect a swimmer's rhythm.

Conclusion

  1. Device reliability was confirmed: 15 active trials and 871.5 seconds of repeated submersion produced no water-ingress or connection problems.
  2. Data completeness was confirmed: the full 6,765-row log was recorded with a stable sampling interval and no missing values.
  3. A first periodic-motion baseline was established: 63–122 cycles per minute and a period CV of 0.22–0.46 provide concrete starting values for window size and noise tolerance.

The pilot also identified two issues early—an unexpectedly low sampling rate and more measurement variability than assumed. Discovering both before involving a real swimmer is precisely why this small experiment was worthwhile.
